Selective-serotonin re-uptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are a scientifically supported option that reduce symptoms for many adults. Typical benefits become noticeable after 4-6 weeks, and about 40-60 % of people experience meaningful improvement. A monitored trial of roughly 6-8 weeks, paired with counselling and healthy-living measures, can clarify effectiveness.

Before starting an SSRI the patient and doctor should:

• Plan for follow-up visits to track benefits and harms.

If the first SSRI is ineffective or poorly tolerated, other SSRIs or different medication classes remain options; one failure does not rule out medication benefit. Should the patient wish to stop, the drug must be tapered gradually under medical supervision to minimise withdrawal symptoms, and treatment may extend longer than originally planned.

Crucially, some individuals hold spiritual or moral convictions that preclude pharmacological treatment. For them, faith-based practices such as prayer, pastoral counselling, and supportive fellowship are valid and should be respected and facilitated. Non-pharmacological approaches—including cognitive-behavioural therapy, exercise, sleep hygiene, and social connection—are useful for anyone, with or without medication.

	Statement 2: Revised Consensus Statement  

Moderate depression deserves timely, active treatment, but whether to start an SSRI is ultimately a personal decision that should be made in partnership with a qualified clinician and with full, tailored information. The jury agrees on the following guiding points:  

1. Evidence and expected benefit  
• SSRIs are among the most-studied treatments for moderate depression and, on average, reduce symptoms by roughly 40–60 %. They rarely provide complete relief on their own, so combining them with psychotherapy, regular physical activity, adequate sleep, and social support usually yields the best outcome.  

2. Time-line and monitoring  
• Therapeutic effects typically emerge after 4–6 weeks. A planned trial of about 6–8 weeks, with regular check-ins, allows the patient and clinician to judge benefit, side-effects, and dosage.  

3. Risks and practical considerations  
• Common early or persistent side-effects include gastrointestinal upset, sleep disturbance, sexual dysfunction, weight change, or transient increases in anxiety; most are manageable but should be discussed up-front.  
• Patients must tell their doctor about all current prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs, supplements, and medical conditions so that dangerous interactions can be avoided.  
• Cost varies; generic SSRIs are usually affordable, whereas some brand-name versions are not. Insurance coverage and financial impact should be reviewed.  

4. Discontinuation and alternative medications  
• Stopping an SSRI generally requires gradual tapering under medical supervision to minimise withdrawal symptoms; starting the medicine should therefore be viewed as a commitment that may extend beyond the initial trial.  
• If the first SSRI is ineffective or poorly tolerated, other SSRIs or different antidepressant classes are available; lack of success with one agent does not rule out benefit from another.  

5. Respect for values and alternative paths  
• The patient’s personal values, cultural background, and spiritual or religious convictions may lead them either toward or away from pharmacological treatment. For some, faith-based, community, or other non-pharmacological approaches are a matter of conscience and should be fully respected and, where desired, supported.  

6. Shared decision-making  
• Whatever option is chosen—SSRI trial, another medication, psychotherapy alone, lifestyle changes, spiritual support, or a tailored combination—ongoing collaboration with healthcare professionals is essential to monitor progress, adjust the plan, and safeguard well-being.
